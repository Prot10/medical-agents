## Source Case (Real Published Case Report)

**Source**: Journal of Medical Case Reports (PMCID: PMC6397472)
**Original diagnosis**: Myasthenia gravis

### Clinical Presentation (from published case):
A 51-year-old woman presented with a 15-day history of sudden onset of reduced tone and texture of her voice. Her voice was feeble but understandable, improving with morning rest and worsening through the day to near-inaudibility. She denied choking, coughing, aspiration, or throat discomfort. She had a long-standing history of constipation but was otherwise healthy, with no tobacco or alcohol use and no family history of chronic illness.

On examination, vital signs were normal. Systemic and neurologic examinations were unremarkable: muscle strength 15/15 on the Medical Research Council scale, normal gait and tandem walking, intact gag reflex and cranial nerves, and normal deep-tendon reflexes. Speech articulation was intact with no scanning, normal tone and resonance, but low intensity and slow rate. An ENT evaluation found no laryngeal pathology, and initial treatment there had no effect.

MRI of the brain and spine was normal. Laboratory studies, including a complete blood count, infectious disease panel, and myopathy panel with creatine kinase, were within normal limits. At this point, other causes having been excluded, clinicians suspected myasthenia gravis.

### Published Diagnostic Reasoning:
1. Laryngeal pathology was considered but excluded — “there was no obvious laryngeal pathology found and the treatment initiated by the ENT department had no satisfying outcome.”  
2. Parkinson’s disease was considered because isolated hypophonia can be an early feature — “hypophonia without obvious dysarthria is more commonly seen in PD with slow but progressive speech intensity decay.” It was ruled out by absence of parkinsonian signs: “She had no imbalance and was able to tandem on walking.”  
3. Bulbar motor neuron disease was considered given the focal voice involvement — “In focal bulbar weakness, MND is suspected first.” It was excluded by normal cranial-nerve and motor examination: “an examination of her gag reflex and other cranial nerves revealed no abnormal reflexes.”  
4. Myasthenia gravis was suspected after exclusion of other diagnoses — “She was suspected to have MG as other diseases were ruled out.”  
5. A neostigmine challengeconfirmed the diagnosis with rapid symptomatic improvement — “there was significant improvement in her voice quality and she felt her fatigability improved as well.”  
6. Acetylcholine-receptor–antibody testing provided definitive confirmation — “the report received after waiting several weeks was positive. Quantitatively, it was significantly higher, 1.11 nmol/l than the normal range (0.0–0.4 nmol/l).”

### 2. SEPARATE presentation from diagnostic evidence
This is critical: the source case text often mentions diagnostic results inline (e.g., "MRI showed periventricular lesions"). You must:
- **REMOVE** diagnostic test results from the HPI and patient presentation
- Instead, place them in the structured **tool outputs** (MRI report, lab results, EEG report, CSF results, ECG)

  - A borderline imaging finding that could suggest an alternative diagnosis
  - A medication that could cause some of the symptoms
  - A family history item that points to a different condition
- **DIAGNOSTIC PUZZLE**: Add significant misdirection:
  - Initial presentation mimics a different condition
  - Key diagnostic test is initially equivocal or negative

  - The critical diagnostic clue is buried in follow-up results, not initial tests
