## Source Case (Real Published Case Report)

**Source**: BMC Neurology (PMCID: PMC4243382)
**Original diagnosis**: Pseudo-subarachnoid hemorrhage

### Clinical Presentation (from published case):
A 37-year-old man presented with a six-month history of progressively worsening, dull occipital headaches radiating bilaterally to the parietal regions. The pain was worst in the mornings (7/10). He had a history of transposition of the great arteries repaired in infancy, multiple pulmonary emboli, and was on long-term phenprocoumon. Non-contrast CT of the head performed at an outside radiology practice showed bilateral hyperattenuations in the cerebral sulci and basal cisterns, and he was referred with a presumed diagnosis of subarachnoid hemorrhage.

On arrival, he was markedly cyanotic with clubbed fingers and hippocratic nails but had a normal neurologic examination. Oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry was 70–74%, which the patient reported as his baseline. Laboratory studies showed a hematocrit of 74.8%, hemoglobin of 22.1 mg/dL, INR of 8.2, and PTT of 114 s. Repeat non-enhanced CT demonstrated the same diffuse sulcal and cisternal hyperdensities. CT angiography of the intracranial vessels revealed no aneurysm or vascular malformation but showed numerous dilated veins in the subarachnoid space.

### Published Diagnostic Reasoning:
1. Subarachnoid hemorrhage was excluded because CTA showed no aneurysm or vascular malformation — “CT-angiography… showed no aneurysm or vascular malformation.”  
2. Purulent meningitis was considered as a leptomeningeal mimic but the patient had no signs of infection — “leptomeningeal disesases such as purulent meningitis…have been reported to mimic SAH.”  
3. Meningeal leukemia and gliomatosis were noted in the literature as SAH mimics but there was no hematologic or imaging evidence — “meningeal leukaemia and gliomatosis have been reported to mimic SAH.”  
4. Contrast agent neurotoxicity can mimic SAH only after contrast administration, which had not occurred in this patient — “Contrast agent neurotoxicity…can only be a differential diagnosis if contrast has been applied.”  
5. Intracranial hypotension can produce pseudo-SAH but the patient lacked clinical or radiologic features of low CSF pressure — “pseudo-SAH has been encountered in patients with intracranial hypotension.”  
6. Cerebellar infarctions and bilateral subdural hematomas are rare mimics yet neither was seen on imaging — “cerebellar infarctions… and bilateral subdural haematoma…”  
7. Generalized cerebral edema from hypoxic encephalopathy can cause sulcal hyperdensities, but there were “no signs of cerebral edema on the CT and MRI scans.”  
8. Chronic polycythaemia increases blood density, and dilated sulcal veins can appear hyperdense, explaining the pseudo-hemorrhage — “density of intravascular blood correlates linearly positively with the haematocrit level” and “proliferated and dilated sulcal and basal veins…had caused the SAH-like picture.”  
9. Susceptibility-weighted MRI confirmed deoxygenated venous blood without evidence of hemorrhage — “SWAN revealed the intracranial veins to be very hypointense…due to the susceptibility effect caused by the increased fraction of deoxyhaemoglobin in the venous blood.”
