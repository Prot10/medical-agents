## Source Case (Real Published Case Report)

**Source**: Case Reports in Psychiatry (PMCID: PMC3745928)
**Original diagnosis**: Delirious mania

### Clinical Presentation (from published case):
• 73-year-old retired accountant with bipolar I disorder (remote manic episodes 12 years prior; one episode of lithium toxicity in 2006).  
• Medications on presentation: lamotrigine 100 mg morning, 200 mg nightly; lithium 600 mg nightly; quetiapine 75 mg nightly (plus PRN); gabapentin; levothyroxine; propranolol (self‐discontinued); perindopril; amlodipine; furosemide.  
• Presented December 9, 2012, with increasing confusion, tremor, rigidity; admitted under neurology for presumed lithium toxicity; lithium held—level 0.75 mmol/L.  
• Serum alcohol and urine toxicology negative; empiric amoxicillin for mixed‐bacteria urinary culture.  
• Psychiatry consulted: initial differential included delirium due to anticholinergic effects or prodrome of bipolar relapse; possible dementia; MMSE 18/30.  
• Transferred December 18 to psychiatric unit; lithium reduced to 150 mg nightly—but florid confusion persisted.  
• Mental status: disorientation to place/time, wandering, disorganized behavior; fluctuating cognition with occasional lucid intervals showing normal speech and occasional racing thoughts; irregular sleep with episodes of lying on floor, mumbling.  
• Workup: CBC, glucose, electrolytes, B12, folate all normal; EEG negative for seizure; chest X-ray, blood and urine cultures negative; no focal neurological or systemic signs—lumbar puncture deemed unnecessary.  
• Brain imaging: CT revealed pituitary mass; MRI confirmed 1.5 cm microadenoma without optic compression; pituitary hormones (cortisol, FSH, LH, prolactin) normal.  
• Thyroid function tests normal (TSH 0.97 mIU/L, free T3 5.1 pmol/L, free T4 16 pmol/L).  
• Medication adjustments: lamotrigine reduced; quetiapine stopped for deliriogenic potential; risperidone 0.25–0.5 mg PRN; brief trial of levodopa/carbidopa for parkinsonism without improvement.  
• Collateral history: baseline high functioning at home with only minor short-term memory issues—dementia ruled out.  
• Four weeks into admission, severe disorientation, agitation, drowsiness persisted despite withdrawal of psychotropics (lithium discontinued January 9; lamotrigine tapered off by January 29).  
• During lucid intervals, observed occasional racing thoughts, slight expansiveness, and mood lability.

### Published Diagnostic Reasoning:
1. Lithium toxicity was considered but excluded because “serum lithium levels were considered to be subtoxic (0.75 mmol/L December 10).”  
2. Urinary tract infection was treated empirically as “amoxicillin was given for a suspected urinary tract infection as he grew mixed bacteria on a urine culture,” yet confusion persisted.  
3. Metabolic causes were excluded since “metabolic abnormalities were ruled out on the basis of normal ranges of complete blood count and serum glucose as well as electrolytes including sodium, potassium calcium, magnesium, chloride, and phosphate.”  
4. Central nervous system infection was deemed unlikely after “a lumbar puncture was considered … but this was deemed unnecessary by neurology … found no focal neurological or systemic signs.”  
5. Substance withdrawal was ruled out because “son’s collateral information of lack of significant substance abuse and dependence history.”  
6. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome and serotonin syndrome were excluded as there was “no pyrexia, autonomic instability, or marked rigidity.”  
7. Dementing syndrome was ruled out based on collateral that he was “relatively high functioning at home with only minor short-term memory issues, with no aphasia, agnosias or visuospatial difficulties.”  
8. Pituitary adenoma effect was excluded since MRI showed “1.5 cm pituitary microadenoma with no optic chiasm compression” and “pituitary hormone values were within normal ranges.”  
9. Hypothyroidism was excluded as contributors given “TSH values of 0.97 mIU/L … Free T3 value of 5.1 pmol/L and free T4 value of 16 pmol/L … hence hypothyroidism was not considered contributing.”  
10. Medication-induced delirium was considered; lamotrigine was decreased and “quetiapine was stopped on December 30 due to its deliriogenic potential.”  
11. Catatonia was contemplated given its association with delirious mania, but he “did not demonstrate the above-mentioned positive or negative classical catatonic signs.”  
12. Delirium alone was considered, but an “extensive medical workup … and excellent response to ECT; hence, delirium as the sole diagnosis is unlikely.”  
13. Evolution of mania superimposed on protracted delirium was considered, but “resolution of both delirious and manic symptoms with ECT … does not support mania evolution superimposed on protracted delirium.”

## Instructions for Seed-Based Case Generation

### 1. USE the real case as your clinical foundation
- The patient demographics, clinical history, presenting symptoms, and exam findings should be **inspired by** the real case
- You may modify demographics (age, sex, ethnicity) slightly for diversity, but keep the clinical scenario realistic and consistent with the source
- The HPI should reflect the same clinical trajectory as the source case, written as a detailed narrative (at least 150 words)
- Preserve the clinical complexity and atypical features from the real case — these are what make it realistic

### 2. SEPARATE presentation from diagnostic evidence
This is critical: the source case text often mentions diagnostic results inline (e.g., "MRI showed periventricular lesions"). You must:
- **REMOVE** diagnostic test results from the HPI and patient presentation
- Instead, place them in the structured **tool outputs** (MRI report, lab results, EEG report, CSF results, ECG)
- The patient presentation should describe symptoms and exam findings, but NOT reveal what the tests showed
- This forces the AI agent to **call diagnostic tools** to discover the evidence

### 3. ADD disguising information and red herrings (especially for moderate/puzzle difficulty)
- **STRAIGHTFORWARD**: Findings clearly support the diagnosis. Minimal confounders.
- **MODERATE**: Add 1-2 incidental/confounding findings:
  - An incidental lab abnormality (e.g., mildly elevated TSH in a stroke patient)
  - A borderline imaging finding that could suggest an alternative diagnosis
  - A medication that could cause some of the symptoms
  - A family history item that points to a different condition
- **DIAGNOSTIC PUZZLE**: Add significant misdirection:
  - Initial presentation mimics a different condition
  - Key diagnostic test is initially equivocal or negative
  - Red herring findings that suggest a more common diagnosis
  - The critical diagnostic clue is buried in follow-up results, not initial tests

### 4. GENERATE structured tool outputs
Create detailed specialist reports for the required modalities. Each report should:
- Read like a real radiologist/pathologist/technician wrote it
- Contain the key diagnostic findings from the source case (now extracted from the narrative)
- Include realistic normal and incidental findings alongside pathological ones
- Have appropriate confidence levels and recommended actions
- Be internally consistent with each other and the patient presentation

### 5. GENERATE follow-up outputs (at least 5)
Create conditional outputs that an agent could request:
- Additional imaging (repeat MRI, CT angiography, DaTscan, VEP, etc.)
- Additional labs (autoimmune panels, genetic testing, specialized markers)

### 6. BUILD ground truth from the source case's reasoning
