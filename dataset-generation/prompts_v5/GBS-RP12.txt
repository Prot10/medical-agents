## Source Case (Real Published Case Report)

**Source**: Journal of Medical Case Reports (PMCID: PMC6642474)
**Original diagnosis**: Guillain-Barré syndrome

### Clinical Presentation (from published case):
A 55-year-old man presented with a 5-day history of numbness and progressive weakness in his upper and lower limbs. He first noted bilateral limb numbness on day 1 and weakness in his toes on day 2, with gradual progression to involve both proximal (grade 4/5) and distal (grade 3/5) muscles of all extremities by admission. He denied respiratory, swallowing, sphincter, or sensory disturbances beyond the numbness and had no recent infections or immunizations. His medical history was notable only for hypertension. 

On examination, higher cortical functions were intact. He had flaccid, symmetric limb weakness with absent deep-tendon reflexes and a flexor plantar response. Sensory, coordination, and sphincter examinations were unremarkable. Cranial-nerve testing revealed isolated partial ptosis of the left eyelid with normal pupil size, reactivity, and extraocular movements; there was no ataxia, diplopia, or fatigability. Vital signs showed blood pressure 160/100 mmHg, pulse 72/min, respiratory rate 12/min, and preserved cough and neck-muscle strength.

Laboratory studies, including complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, electrolytes (potassium, calcium, magnesium), liver and renal function tests, were all within normal limits. Nerve conduction studies demonstrated a severe demyelinating polyneuropathy. Repetitive nerve stimulation showed no decremental response, and single-muscle-fiber electromyography was negative. An ice pack test did not improve the ptosis.

### Published Diagnostic Reasoning:
1. Miller Fisher syndrome was considered but was unlikely given the absence of ophthalmoplegia or ataxia and a demyelinating rather than axonal pattern on nerve conduction studies — “Our patient never developed either ophthalmoplegia or ataxia during his illness. Furthermore, his nerve conduction studies showed a demyelinating pattern rather than an axonal pattern, which is seen in Miller Fisher syndrome.”  
2. Myasthenia gravis was excluded due to lack of fatigability or diurnal variation, normal repetitive nerve stimulation and single-fiber EMG, and no response to ice pack testing — “it was excluded in our patient owing to the absence of fatigability or diurnal variation of symptoms, and further, no decremental response to repetitive nerve stimulation was observed, and the result of single-muscle-fiber EMG was negative. … the patient’s ptosis did not improve with the ice pack test.”  
3. Botulism was ruled out because of normal pupillary function, absence of ophthalmoplegia, and an ascending rather than descending pattern of paralysis — “it was excluded in the presence of normal pupillary function and in the absence of ophthalmoplegia. Also, descending paralysis is a feature of botulism rather than ascending paralysis, which was observed in our patient.”  
4. A focal brainstem lesion was considered unlikely in the context of normal brainstem function, preserved higher cortical and bulbar function, and unremarkable initial imaging — “Normal MRI findings other than the contrast enhancement … excluded the possibility of brainstem diseases.”  
5. Guillain-Barré syndrome was supported by the clinical picture of acute ascending flaccid paralysis, severe demyelinating polyneuropathy on nerve conduction studies, and the later finding of CSF protein cell dissociation typical of GBS — “CSF analysis typically reveals elevated protein concentrations without pleocytosis in GBS, as in our patient’s case.”

### 1. USE the real case as your clinical foundation
- The patient demographics, clinical history, presenting symptoms, and exam findings should be **inspired by** the real case

- The HPI should reflect the same clinical trajectory as the source case, written as a detailed narrative (at least 150 words)
- Preserve the clinical complexity and atypical features from the real case — these are what make it realistic

### 2. SEPARATE presentation from diagnostic evidence
This is critical: the source case text often mentions diagnostic results inline (e.g., "MRI showed periventricular lesions"). You must:
- **REMOVE** diagnostic test results from the HPI and patient presentation
- Instead, place them in the structured **tool outputs** (MRI report, lab results, EEG report, CSF results, ECG)
- The patient presentation should describe symptoms and exam findings, but NOT reveal what the tests showed
- This forces the AI agent to **call diagnostic tools** to discover the evidence

### 3. ADD disguising information and red herrings (especially for moderate/puzzle difficulty)
- **STRAIGHTFORWARD**: Findings clearly support the diagnosis. Minimal confounders.
- **MODERATE**: Add 1-2 incidental/confounding findings:
  - An incidental lab abnormality (e.g., mildly elevated TSH in a stroke patient)
  - A borderline imaging finding that could suggest an alternative diagnosis
  - A medication that could cause some of the symptoms
  - A family history item that points to a different condition
- **DIAGNOSTIC PUZZLE**: Add significant misdirection:
  - Initial presentation mimics a different condition
  - Key diagnostic test is initially equivocal or negative
  - Red herring findings that suggest a more common diagnosis
  - The critical diagnostic clue is buried in follow-up results, not initial tests

### 4. GENERATE structured tool outputs
Create detailed specialist reports for the required modalities. Each report should:
- Read like a real radiologist/pathologist/technician wrote it
- Contain the key diagnostic findings from the source case (now extracted from the narrative)
- Include realistic normal and incidental findings alongside pathological ones

- Be internally consistent with each other and the patient presentation
