INTERACTIVE PAPER EXPLAINER

Can an AI doctor survive a real conversation?

A guided, grounded walkthrough of AgentClinic: a multimodal benchmark for tool-using clinical AI agents.

Large language models now ace medical licensing exams. This paper asks a sharper question: what happens when you stop handing the model a tidy exam question — and instead make it talk to a patient, order its own tests, read scans, and commit to a diagnosis, all under a turn limit? The answer is sobering, and that gap is what this study is built to measure.

1 The problem

A 90% exam score that means surprisingly little

Here is a fact that sounds like a finish line.

In September 2021, the best language model scored 38.1% on the . By November 2023 — barely two years later — that number had climbed to 90.2%. For comparison, the human passing score is 60% and the human expert score is 87%. On paper, the machine had overtaken the expert.

By the end of this section you'll understand why a high medical-exam score does not mean a model is ready for clinical work — and what is missing from the test.

So why isn't the problem solved? Because of how that exam is scored. The USMLE — and the dataset built from such questions — is static multiple choice. The model receives a complete in which every relevant fact is already laid out, then picks the most plausible answer from a list.

Real clinical work looks nothing like that. The paper calls it a multiplexed task built on sequential decision-making: a clinician must handle uncertainty with limited information and finite resources, decide which question to ask next, order the right test at the right time, and draw relevant information out of a patient — compassionately. None of that is exercised when the answer key is already on the page.

"Existing benchmarks rely heavily on static question-answering, which does not accurately depict the complex, sequential nature of clinical decision-making."

And this is not an abstract worry. Over 40% of the global population faces limited access to healthcare, and health systems are strained — so models that could genuinely scale clinical reasoning would matter enormously. That makes it essential to know whether the exam score is real capability or an artefact of the format.

The paper's headline test of that question: take the same MedQA questions and re-cast them as interactive, conversation-driven cases. The result —

★ Key takeaway
A static multiple-choice exam hands the model every fact and asks it to recognise an answer. Real medicine asks a clinician to gather the facts first. When the paper re-cast MedQA questions in that harder, sequential form, accuracy dropped — for the weakest models, to below a tenth of their original score. The exam was measuring the wrong thing.
Self-check
What is the core limitation of static multiple-choice medical benchmarks, according to the paper?
Correct: the second option. The paper's objection is structural — a vignette lays out every fact and asks the model only to select an answer. It never exercises the sequential, uncertain, information-gathering work that defines real clinical practice.
2 The big idea

AgentClinic: turn the exam into a clinic

By the end of this section you'll understand what AgentClinic is — a simulated clinical environment — and how it differs from a benchmark that just asks questions.

If the problem is the static format, the fix is to build something dynamic. AgentClinic is an open-source, multimodal benchmark that simulates a clinical environment using language agents.

Instead of being handed a vignette, the model under test is dropped into the role of a doctor. It starts with almost nothing — just a one-line objective, such as "Evaluate the patient presenting with chest pain, palpitations, and shortness of breath." From there it must interview a simulated patient, order physical exams and lab tests, request medical scans, and finally commit to a diagnosis — all within a limited number of conversational turns.

Three design choices make this more than a chatbot demo:

  • It is multi-agent. The patient, the test results, and the grading are each played by their own language agent. (You'll meet all four in the next section.)
  • It is multimodal. Many real diagnoses need eyes — a rash, an X-ray, an MRI. AgentClinic lets the doctor request and read .
  • It is grounded in real cases. The scenarios are built from real medical-exam questions, real , and real published diagnostic puzzles — not invented stories.

On top of that environment, the authors add two research instruments: a way to inject 23 known clinical biases into the agents, and a toolbox of 6 tools the doctor can be given. Both turn AgentClinic from a single test into a flexible laboratory — and both get their own sections later.

The whole thing is packaged as five sub-benchmarks, each stressing a different skill:

AgentClinic replaces "answer this question" with "run this consultation." It is a simulated clinic — multi-agent, multimodal, and built from real cases — where the model must earn its diagnosis by interacting, not recognise it from a list.
3 The cast

Four agents, one of them on trial

By the end of this section you'll be able to name all four language agents, say what each one does, and identify which single agent is actually being graded.
Key terms first
  • Language agent — a large language model given a fixed role and private instructions, so it behaves as one participant in the simulation.
  • Interaction turn — one round of the simulation: one message from the doctor, plus one reply from the patient, a tool, or the measurement agent.

AgentClinic runs on four language agents. Each gets its own instructions and its own slice of the case file — and crucially, no agent can see information meant for another. Click each agent below to see its role.

Enable JavaScript for the interactive agent panel. The four agents are: the Doctor (the model under test), the Patient (gives symptoms and history but not the diagnosis), the Measurement agent (returns realistic test and scan results on request), and the Moderator (compares the doctor's final diagnosis to the ground truth and scores it).

Who is actually being tested?

This is the point students most often miss. Only the doctor agent is the subject of the experiment — it is "the model whose performance is being evaluated." The other three exist purely to create the test environment. The patient supplies the symptoms, the measurement agent supplies the data, and the moderator supplies the grade.

That design also means the doctor never has an unfair shortcut: it begins with only a one-line objective and must extract everything else through dialogue and test orders, one interaction turn at a time, up to a turn budget (the default is N = 20).

Grounded in Figure 1 and the Methods section ("Agent Details").
Self-check
In an AgentClinic simulation, which agent's performance is the experiment actually measuring?
Correct: the doctor agent. It is the model on trial. The patient, measurement, and moderator agents are the apparatus — they generate the case, supply data on request, and grade the outcome, but they are not what the benchmark scores.
★ Key takeaway
Four agents, one subject. The doctor is graded; the patient, measurement, and moderator agents form the environment around it. Information is deliberately partitioned — the doctor must work for every fact it gets.

In summary: the doctor greets the patient → the patient reports a severe week-long headache and trouble moving the eyes → the doctor takes a history → the doctor requests an MRI of the brain and orbits → the measurement agent returns the scan → the doctor diagnoses cavernous sinus thrombosis → the moderator compares it to the ground truth and marks it correct.

What to notice

Three things in that episode are the whole point of the benchmark:

  • The doctor was never told the diagnosis. It had to be assembled from the conversation.
  • The MRI did not appear automatically — the doctor had to decide it was needed and explicitly request it. A wasted or missing test costs a turn.
  • The episode ends with a separate moderator comparing free-text output to . The doctor doesn't grade itself.
★ Key takeaway
A simulation is a chain of decisions: greet → interview → decide what to test → order it → interpret → diagnose → get graded. Every link is a place the model can succeed or fail — and none of them exist in a multiple-choice question.
5 The raw material

Where the cases come from

By the end of this section you'll know the real-world sources behind AgentClinic's cases and how a raw medical question becomes a four-agent simulation.

A benchmark is only as trustworthy as its cases. AgentClinic deliberately avoids invented scenarios and instead draws from three kinds of real medical material: medical-exam questions (USMLE / MedQA), de-identified electronic health records (MIMIC-IV), and published diagnostic puzzles (NEJM case challenges), plus specialist case reports from MedMCQA.

From a question to a simulation

The conversion follows a fixed recipe. A real question is selected, then poured into a structured JSON template — and that template splits the information across the four agents:

  • the brief objective → the doctor agent;
  • the patient history & symptoms → the patient agent;
  • the physical-exam & test findings → the measurement agent;
  • the correct diagnosis → the moderator agent only.

That partition is what forces genuine information-gathering: no single agent holds the whole picture.

6 Result #1

The same questions, suddenly much harder

By the end of this section you'll understand the paper's central result — that sequential, interactive diagnosis is dramatically harder than static QA — and that exam scores don't predict it.

On MedQA's static multiple-choice format, the strongest models score in the high 80s and low 90s. So before you see the interactive results, commit to a guess:

The best model scores ~90%+ on static MedQA. What does the best model score on AgentClinic-MedQA — the interactive version?
The best model — Claude-3.5 Sonnet — scores 62.1% ± 3.3. The very best performer loses roughly thirty percentage points just by having to conduct the consultation instead of reading it. And it falls away fast from there.

Three things jump out of that chart:

  • Claude-3.5 Sonnet leads both benchmarks — 62.1% on MedQA-cases, 42.9% on real records. The paper finds Claude-sourced agents outperform other backbones "in most settings."
  • Human physicians sit mid-pack at 54% — but with a huge ±28.5 spread, because the human panel was only 3 clinicians. Read that number cautiously.
  • The floor is brutal. Llama-2-70B-chat manages just 4.5% on AgentClinic-MedQA. This is the abstract's "below a tenth of the original accuracy" in the flesh.

Exam scores don't predict clinic scores

The most consequential finding is about ranking. The paper compares each model's static MedQA score with its AgentClinic-MedQA score (its Figure 3) and concludes that MedQA accuracy is only weakly predictive of AgentClinic-MedQA accuracy. A model can be excellent at the exam and mediocre in the simulated clinic; the ordering reshuffles.

The authors draw a pointed analogy from medical education itself: the USMLE is known to be a poor predictor of how a doctor later performs as a clinical resident. The static test and the real job measure different things — for machines just as for humans.

"MedQA accuracy is not predictive of accuracy on AgentClinic-MedQA."
★ Key takeaway
Making the task interactive doesn't just lower every score by a fixed amount — it reshuffles the leaderboard. A strong exam score is no longer evidence of clinical competence. To know how a model handles a consultation, you have to test it in a consultation.
Self-check
"MedQA accuracy is not predictive of AgentClinic-MedQA accuracy" — what does this mean in practice?
Correct: the second option. If the drop were uniform, the exam score would still predict the clinic score. The point is that it isn't uniform — models reorder — so a high MedQA score tells you little about interactive performance. (The questions are actually the same; only the format changes.)
7 Result #2

The consultation itself is a skill

By the end of this section you'll see three ways the conversation — its length, its thoroughness, and who plays the patient — directly drives the diagnosis.

If diagnosis is sequential, then the sequence should matter. The paper shows it does, in three distinct ways.

1 · How many turns the doctor gets

Each simulation caps the doctor at N interaction turns. The authors varied N and re-measured accuracy. Predict first: if you halved the turn budget from 20 to 10, what would happen — and is more always better? Then drag the slider.

52% diagnostic accuracy at N = 20 turns
1015202530

The curve is not a straight line. Cutting the budget hurts hard — N=10 collapses to 25%, less than half the N=20 score — because the doctor simply runs out of turns before it has gathered enough information. But pushing past 20 also hurts slightly (N=25 → 48%, N=30 → 43%): the paper suggests the ever-growing conversation becomes harder for the model to process. There is a sweet spot, and it is around N = 20.

2 · How much the doctor actually finds out

The paper introduces a metric called — the share of the relevant patient information the doctor managed to extract. Using GPT-4 as the doctor, average coverage on AgentClinic-MedQA was 67%. And it splits cleanly by outcome:

Information coverage vs. diagnostic outcome (GPT-4 doctor)
Correct diagnosis
72%
Incorrect diagnosis
63%
When the doctor got the diagnosis right it had uncovered 72% of the relevant facts; when wrong, only 63%. Gathering information is the job.

3 · Who plays the patient

Here is a subtle one. The patient is also an LLM — and which model plays it changes the doctor's score. With GPT-4 as the doctor, accuracy was 52% against a GPT-4 patient, 48% against a GPT-3.5 patient, and 46% against a Mixtral patient.

Why? Behaviour. GPT-3.5 patients tend to simply echo the doctor's question back without adding anything:

Doctor: Have you experienced any muscle twitching or cramps?
Patient (GPT-3.5): No, I haven't experienced any muscle twitching or cramps.

GPT-4 patients more often volunteer genuinely new clinical detail, surfacing clues a doctor can act on. There is even a stranger effect: a GPT-3.5 doctor scores about 37% with a GPT-3.5 patient but about 38% with a GPT-4 patient — talking across different model families is itself a measurable extra challenge.

The authors' practical advice follows directly: never evaluate a doctor model against a single patient model — test it against a panel of patient agents of varying ability.

★ Key takeaway
Three levers, all part of "the conversation": turn budget (too few starves the doctor, too many overwhelms it — best near N=20), coverage (right answers come from finding more facts — 72% vs 63%), and the patient model (a better-acting patient lifts the doctor's score). Conducting the consultation well is a skill in its own right.
Self-check
Accuracy was 52% at N=20 but only 48% at N=25 and 43% at N=30. Why does giving the doctor more turns slightly hurt?
Correct: the second option. The paper attributes the dip past N=20 to "the growing input size, which can be difficult for language models." More turns mean a longer transcript to reason over — eventually a liability, not a help.
8 The toolbox

Give the doctor tools — does it help?

By the end of this section you'll know the 6 tools AgentClinic can hand the doctor, and the paper's surprising finding that tools do not help every model.

A real clinician doesn't reason from memory alone — they look things up, take notes, learn from past cases. AgentClinic gives the doctor agent an Agent Toolbox of six tools. Expand each to see how it works.

🧩Zero-Shot CoTReason step by step+
prompting: the model is told to articulate its reasoning step by step before answering — with no worked example provided.
🧩One-Shot CoTReason, with one example+
Same idea, but the prompt includes one worked example (k=1), chosen from a repository of previously solved cases by semantic similarity to the current problem.
🔁Reflection CoTLearn from past successes+
An loop in four steps:
  1. Verification — the final answer is checked against ground truth.
  2. Reflection — if correct, the model distils its winning reasoning into a concise "takeaway."
  3. Memory storage — the takeaway + original case are stored in a vector database.
  4. Experiential retrieval — on a new case, the most similar past takeaways are pulled back in as dynamic examples.
No weights change — it's in-context learning, distinct from static retrieval.
📓NotebookPersistent notes across cases+
The doctor can write and edit free-form tips that persist from one patient to the next.
"[Note #17] Remember that timing and onset of symptoms can provide valuable diagnostic insights."
📚Adaptive RAG (Book)Search medical textbooks+
from 18 medical textbooks used by US medical students. "Adaptive" means the model itself decides when and what to retrieve.
Research textbooks 'What are the symptoms of myasthenia gravis?'
🌐Adaptive RAG (Web)Search online sources+
Adaptive retrieval from an internet database — PubMed research articles, StatPearls, and medical Wikipedia articles.

The twist: tools are not universally good

You might assume tools can only help. They don't. The paper's striking result is that the same tool helps one model and hurts another:

  • Claude-3.5 — strongest with tools overall (averaging 51.3%), peaking at 56.1% with the Notebook.
  • GPT-4 — gains modestly; best at 43.9% with Adaptive RAG (Web) and 42.2% with Reflection CoT.
  • GPT-4o — best at 43.0% with the Notebook.
  • GPT-3.5 — worse on every single tool; Adaptive RAG (Book) caused a 27.1% drop.
  • Llama-3-70B — consistent gains (≈9.4% average); the abstract reports up to a 92% relative improvement with the Notebook tool.

Picking up a tool and getting value from it are two different abilities. A model can be handed a textbook search and still do worse — retrieving the wrong thing, or being distracted by it.

⚑ Grounding note — the Llama-3 number
The abstract and contributions state Llama-3 shows "up to 92% relative improvements" with the Notebook; the Discussion section instead phrases the tool gains as "up to 19.7% improvement." The two figures appear in different parts of the same paper. The headline 92% is a relative gain over a low baseline; both are reported here as the paper states them.
★ Key takeaway
AgentClinic treats tool use as its own axis of evaluation. Tools are not free wins — they helped Claude and Llama-3 substantially, did little for GPT-4, and actively hurt GPT-3.5. Knowing how to use a tool well is a capability that separates models.
9 Result #3

What happens when you add bias

By the end of this section you'll understand how AgentClinic injects 23 clinical biases, why one model shrugged them off while another buckled, and how bias quietly damages the patient's experience.
Key terms first
  • — a systematic error of reasoning, e.g. recency or anchoring bias.
  • — unconscious associations tied to social groups (race, gender, education, and others).
  • — accuracy with a bias ÷ accuracy without it. 100% means the bias did no damage.

AgentClinic can seed 23 distinct biases into the system prompt of the doctor agent, the patient agent, or both. A cognitive bias might tell a doctor it recently saw a similar patient (recency bias); an implicit bias might shape how a patient trusts a doctor based on a social characteristic. The question: how much does that degrade care?

One model shrugged; one model buckled

The paper tested two doctor models under bias. Toggle between them — each bar shows normalized accuracy (closer to 100% = more robust).

0%25%50%75%100%

GPT-4 barely moved — across all introduced biases its accuracy fell by at most 4 percentage points (1.5% on average). The paper offers a curious explanation: GPT-4 may be over-aligned. In 25 of 215 gender-bias dialogues it explicitly refused to act on the biased instruction and stated it would judge objectively. That "bias-rejection" makes GPT-4 robust — but also a poor stand-in for studying how bias actually plays out.

Mixtral-8x7B buckled. Its worst case — a cognitive bias on the doctor — drove normalized accuracy down to 78.4% (37% → 29%). Reviewing those failures, the authors saw the model fixate on a recent diagnosis and ignore new symptoms (recency bias), and grow reluctant to order necessary tests for patients carrying a racial-bias prompt — a simulated disparity in care. That makes Mixtral the more informative model for studying bias.

Bias hurts the patient, not just the score

AgentClinic also captures something a pure accuracy number misses. After each consultation the patient agent gives three 1–10 ratings: confidence in the doctor, compliance (will they follow the treatment?), and consultation (will they come back?).

When only the patient was biased, the paper found that implicit biases damaged all three ratings — education bias most consistently of all. Among cognitive biases, a patient's self-diagnosis bias hit hardest, dropping confidence by 4.7 points and consultation by 2. Bias doesn't just cause a wrong diagnosis; it erodes the trust that makes treatment work.

Read this carefully
These ratings come from simulated patient agents, not humans. There is no defined meaning for "a 6 vs a 7," and no inter-rater reliability analysis. The paper stresses they show relative differences (biased vs unbiased), not a true measure of human perception.
★ Key takeaway
Bias resilience is model-specific: GPT-4 resisted bias (perhaps by over-refusing it), while Mixtral-8x7B degraded sharply. And bias is not only an accuracy problem — it lowers a patient's confidence, compliance, and willingness to return. Robustness to bias must be tested directly, across many biases.
Self-check
Why do the authors suggest Mixtral-8x7B may be a better model than GPT-4 for studying bias?
Correct: the second option. GPT-4's robustness may be over-alignment — it explicitly rejects biased prompts. A model that won't display bias can't be used to study it. Mixtral's larger, visible degradation makes the mechanism observable.
10 Breadth

Across languages and specialties

By the end of this section you'll know how performance varies across 7 languages and 9 specialties — and why dialogue reshuffles which specialties look "easy."

Seven languages

AgentClinic-Lang tests the same clinical reasoning in English, Chinese, French, Spanish, Hindi, Persian, and Korean. The pattern is stark: every model performed best in English, and most fell off a cliff elsewhere.

  • Claude-3.5 Sonnet was the clear exception — consistently strong across all seven, averaging 48.4%, more than double the next-best model (GPT-4 at 20.9%).
  • GPT-4 ranged from 11.21% (Chinese) to 40.18% (English).
  • GPT-4o ranged from 3.73% (Korean) to 35.5% (English).

For a tool meant to widen healthcare access, a benchmark that only works in English would miss the point — which is exactly why this sub-benchmark exists.

Nine specialties

AgentClinic-Spec spreads cases across nine specialties. Claude-3.5 again led, averaging 66.7% — strongest in Internal Medicine (78.3%), Otolaryngology (76.7%), and Gynecology (74.3%). GPT-4 was strong in Gynecology (68.5%) and Ophthalmology (65.2%) but weak in Emergency Medicine (32.3%) and Geriatrics (40%).

The interesting twist: in the static medical-QA literature, psychiatry and otolaryngology are usually rated among the least challenging specialties. In AgentClinic's dialogue-driven format, that ordering does not hold — Internal Medicine and Gynecology come out higher. Diagnosing through a conversation changes which specialties are hard.

★ Key takeaway
Performance is uneven across both languages (all models favour English; only Claude-3.5 stays robust) and specialties (and the "hard" ones differ from static-QA expectations). Breadth testing exposes gaps a single English general-medicine benchmark would hide.
11 Result #4

When the diagnosis needs eyes

By the end of this section you'll understand the multimodal benchmark — diagnosing from medical images — and why it matters whether the model is handed the image or must ask for it.

Many diagnoses can't be reached by conversation alone. A rash, an infection, an X-ray, a CT, an MRI — these need to be seen. The paper notes hospitalized patients receive an average of 1.42 diagnostic images per stay. So AgentClinic-NEJM tests four vision-capable models on 120 image-based cases from NEJM case challenges, scored open-ended (the multiple-choice options exist but are hidden from the doctor).

Handed the image vs. having to ask for it

The clever design choice: images can reach the doctor two ways — provided up front at the start, or only on request from the measurement agent. The second is more realistic (a clinician must decide imaging is needed) and slightly harder. Toggle to compare:

0%25%50%75%100%

Two observations. First, Claude-3.5 Sonnet again leads (37.2% up-front, 35.4% on request) and GPT-4o-mini trails badly (8.0% / 6.1%) — multimodal medical reasoning is genuinely hard, and the scores are low across the board. Second, every model drops a little when it must request the image itself — adding the decision step costs accuracy.

One encouraging detail: when GPT-4 gave a wrong open-ended answer, the correct diagnosis was still among the (hidden) multiple-choice options 60% of the time. The model is often in the right neighbourhood — but open-ended diagnosis, with no list to pick from, is far less forgiving.

★ Key takeaway
Real diagnosis is multimodal, and current models are weak at it — best-in-class Claude-3.5 still only reaches ~37%. Making the model decide to order an image, rather than handing it over, costs a few more points. Open-ended image diagnosis is much harder than picking from a list.

13 Honest accounting

What this can't do — and what it means

By the end of this section you'll be able to state the study's main limitations and its three concrete recommendations for evaluating clinical AI.

Limitations the authors name

  • A simplified clinic. Only four roles. Real care also involves nurses, family members, administrators, and insurers — none are modelled yet.
  • A tiny human baseline. Just 3 clinicians for scoring and rating — not generalisable.
  • Possible data leakage. GPT-4 and Claude-3.5 may have seen MedQA during training. The authors argue that focusing on novel interaction patterns (query frequency, tool-use sequences) makes the findings more robust to this — but they cannot fully rule it out.
  • The moderator is itself an LLM judge. It agrees with humans over 80% of the time (about the level humans agree with each other), but the sensitivity of results to the moderator's own model and prompt was not explored.
  • The measurement agent can hallucinate. Because it is an LLM generating test values, it may introduce errors; a real database or SQL tool could fix this.
  • Tool commands are abstractions. "Order an MRI" is one command here; in a real hospital it is many steps, people, and constraints. The authors call this a deliberate simplification that limits ecological validity.

Three recommendations for evaluating clinical AI

The study converts its findings into concrete advice for anyone benchmarking a medical model:

  1. Don't use a single patient agent. A doctor's score depends on who plays the patient — evaluate against a panel of varying ability.
  2. Test bias resilience deliberately. Some models hold up, others collapse — run a whole suite of cognitive and implicit biases.
  3. Treat tool use as a core axis. Effective use of retrieval and notebooks is a real, model-distinguishing capability — measure it.
"We believe that LLMs need to be examined with novel evaluation strategies that go well beyond static question-answering benchmarks."

That sentence is the paper in one line. AgentClinic itself — earlier benchmarks like AMIE, SAPS and CRAFT-MD did dialogue but lacked multimodal input, real-world bias, tool use, multilingual or specialist coverage; MedAgents looked at agent collaboration but not patient interaction or tools. AgentClinic's contribution is to put all of that into one interactive, multimodal environment.

★ Key takeaway
The paper is candid: a simplified clinic, a small human panel, an LLM-based moderator, possible data leakage. Its lasting message survives all of that — clinical AI must be tested in dynamic, interactive, multimodal conditions, because static exam scores measure the wrong thing.

The argument in seven beats

  • 1 · The trap. Models score ~90% on static medical exams, but those exams hand over every fact and only test recognition.
  • 2 · The fix. AgentClinic simulates a clinic: a doctor agent must interview, test, image, and diagnose — within a turn limit.
  • 3 · The cast. Four agents — doctor (graded), patient, measurement, moderator — with information deliberately partitioned.
  • 4 · The collapse. Re-cast as interactive cases, accuracy plunges; the best model reaches only 62.1%, and exam rank no longer predicts clinic rank.
  • 5 · The conversation. Turn budget, information coverage, and the patient model each move the score — conducting a consultation is a skill.
  • 6 · Tools & bias. Tools help some models and hurt others; bias degrades some models badly and quietly erodes patient trust.
  • 7 · The verdict. Evaluate clinical AI dynamically — multimodal, multilingual, tool-using, bias-tested — not with static QA.

Final self-check

Review question 1 of 3
A new model scores 91% on MedQA. Based on this paper, what can you safely conclude about its AgentClinic-MedQA performance?
Correct: the third option. The paper's core finding is that MedQA accuracy does not predict AgentClinic-MedQA accuracy. The only way to know is to run the interactive benchmark.
Review question 2 of 3
Which statement about the agent toolbox matches the paper's findings?
Correct: the second option. Tool benefit is model-specific. Claude-3.5 peaked at 56.1% with the Notebook and Llama-3 gained sharply, while GPT-3.5 declined on all six tools.
Review question 3 of 3
Why does the paper measure patient confidence, compliance, and consultation ratings — not just diagnostic accuracy?
Correct: the first option. These patient-centric metrics capture trust, compliance, and willingness to return — dimensions of care quality that pure accuracy ignores. (Remember the caveat: they come from simulated agents, so they show relative differences, not true human perception.)
