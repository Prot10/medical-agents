## Changes, in one paragraph a physician can read before re-reviewing
A 29-year-old neuroscience graduate student who handles organophosphates is found comatose on a sidewalk with an open organophosphate container in his backpack. The presentation is now a cholinergic crisis that matches the label: pinpoint pupils, drenching sweat, lacrimation, bronchorrhea with wheeze, vomiting and incontinence, fasciculations of the face, chest wall and calves, tachycardia 128 (a red herring that should not deter atropine), BP 94/56, SpO2 88% and 38.4 C. The core labs show markedly reduced plasma and RBC cholinesterase, a high anion gap lactic acidosis with inadequate respiratory compensation and hypoxemia, potassium 2.8, glucose 186, CK 8640 with creatinine 1.6 and a negative routine drug screen; the CT is normal; the ECG shows QTc 492 ms with U waves. The continuous video-EEG separates two things: nine bifrontal-onset seizures in the first hour (electrographic status epilepticus) and continuous twitching without EEG correlate (fasciculations). The post-treatment EEG, the 12-hour cholinesterase recovery panel, the 48-hour nerve conduction study, the respiratory function test in a comatose patient, the orphan LP and the fabricated trial are gone; the drug check is recommended and lists real interactions, and literature is optional. Musts: decontamination, atropine to a dry chest regardless of the tachycardia, pralidoxime by the WHO regimen with its uncertainty stated, intubation with rocuronium, a benzodiazepine and then levetiracetam (not fosphenytoin while hypotensive), potassium and magnesium for the QTc, fluids for rhabdomyolysis, cEEG, and ICU monitoring for the intermediate syndrome before extubation.
