\section{Results (2,350 words, six subsections)}\label{sec:results}

\role{Turn the thesis into evidence in the order a sceptic needs it: trust the object (R1), see the gap
(R2), see the fix (R3), see that physicians agree (R4), see that it leaves the simulator (R5), see where
the gain comes from (R6).}

\noindent\textbf{Rules for every subsection.} Open with the claim as a sentence; one figure; effect sizes
with 95\% CIs, $n$ in cases, the test used; the case is the unit, seeds averaged within case; the
NeuroBench test carries the primary comparison and MIMIC-IV the external one, never pooled; every
effect on the test is also given per condition, because the test holds four conditions; method
details go to Methods.

\noindent\textbf{One name per measure.} Correct diagnoses (\%); required tests ordered (\%); ordered
tests the policy asks for (\%), the share of a model's tests that match a required or recommended item
of the case policy; tests per case, read against the number the case policies ask for and against
physicians; cost relative to the required work-up, the cost of the tests ordered over the cost of the
required tests of the case (1 means the model spends what the case needs). Cases with an unsafe action
and useless tests per case stay in Extended Data Table~1.

\subsection{R1. An executable clinical-policy benchmark for local agents}\label{sec:benchmark}

\role{The reader must trust the object before any result trained on it; reviewers look here for leakage
and simulator artefacts.}

\content
\begin{itemize}
\item Episode: presentation, one typed action per turn (16 tools), stored results, a forced final
assessment.
\item Policy and score: required, recommended and avoided actions, order, accepted diagnoses; the policy
score from 0 to 1 with caps; perfect-agent check as proof of internal consistency.
\item Dataset: 600 synthetic cases of 20 conditions split by whole condition (419 / 60 / 119); review by
two neurologists, the corrections made from their notes and the re-checked sample, inter-physician
agreement; 100 MIMIC-IV admissions for testing only.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{R2. A correct diagnosis can hide an incomplete work-up}\label{sec:outcome}

outcome alone is a problem worth solving.}

\content
\begin{itemize}
\item Untrained panel: Qwen3.5-9B, Granite 4.1 8B, Gemma 4 E4B and two or three larger models, on the
119 test cases, three seeds. Models only: physicians are compared with the models in Fig.~4.
\item Correct diagnoses against required tests ordered per model.
\item What lies behind correct diagnoses: complete work-up, complete with tests the policy does not ask
for, required test missed.
\item Why a test counts as required when the diagnosis can be read from the history: it confirms the
diagnosis and rules out the alternatives that would change the treatment. One sentence here, the
evidence in Fig.~4c (the neurologists' own share of required tests).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{R3. Rewarding the work-up teaches it without losing diagnoses}\label{sec:rl}

\role{Claim C2: the causal test of the thesis. Everything else supports this subsection, so it gets the
most words.}

\content
\begin{itemize}
\item Design: two models trained from the same untrained Qwen3.5-9B with GRPO, differing only in the
reward: the policy score, or diagnosis and cost (weights 0.85 and 0.15, our choice: SDBench reports
the two measures and defines no reward). Same cases, seeds and budget; each checkpoint chosen on
validation by its own reward. A control trained on the diagnosis alone (the outcome reward without
its cost term) shows whether the cost term is what cuts the testing.
\item Test: the 119 cases of four conditions never seen in training, paired by case. Correct diagnoses,
required tests ordered, ordered tests the policy asks for, tests per case against the number the case
policies ask for, cost relative to the required work-up, and both scores, so each model is judged
also on the reward it was not trained on.
\item What to expect from the first seed (validation cases, draft policies): the outcome-reward model
keeps its diagnoses and orders about half the tests of the untrained model, so it saves by leaving
required tests out; the policy-reward model doubles the required tests ordered at the cost of the
untrained model.
\item Right tests or just more tests: a scripted agent that orders the same $k$ tests in every case,
those most often required in the training cases, for every $k$ up to all of them. The trained model
must sit above that line.
\item Statistics: Wilcoxon and paired bootstrap; McNemar for proportions; Benjamini--Hochberg; every
effect per condition.
\end{itemize}

\structure
\begin{enumerate}
\item Design in two sentences, the two rewards (100 words).
\item What each model learns in training, Fig.~3a (80).
\item Main effect on the test cases, Fig.~3b and ED Table~1 (200).
\item Per condition, Fig.~3c (80).
\item Right tests, not just more tests, Fig.~3d (100).
\item Cost: the model spends where the case needs it (40).
\end{enumerate}

\artefacts
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fig.~3} (below, revised 18 Nov after the second critical review, awaiting approval).
\textbf{ED Table~1}: every number with CIs and $P$.
\item \textbf{ED Fig.~2} reward history, with the choice of the penalty for a test the policy does not
ask for; \textbf{ED Fig.~4} other models; \textbf{ED Fig.~6} acute vs chronic; \textbf{ED Fig.~8}
robustness; \textbf{ED Fig.~9} a second split with other conditions held out. \textbf{Supp.\
Tables~3--4} per condition (the numbers of Fig.~3c), controls and scripted agents (Fig.~3d).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{R4. Physicians agree with the policy score}\label{sec:validity}

\role{Claim C3. Without it the policy score is our opinion, and almost every measure of Fig.~3 comes
from the policy itself. The blind rating is the test of the main effect that uses no score.}

\content
\begin{itemize}
\item Both studies run on MIMIC-IV admissions, which the neurologists have never seen (they reviewed
the synthetic cases). At least one neurologist who has not seen the case policies takes part.
\item Blind rating of $\sim$100 work-ups (agent hidden) on a fixed scale; Spearman $\rho$ of the
ratings with the policy score, the outcome score, diagnosis alone, the number of tests ordered and an
LLM judge, against the agreement between two neurologists. The count of tests is the rival to beat:
if neurologists only like more tests, the policy score adds nothing to it.
\item The blind rating of each agent's work-ups: untrained, multi-agent panel (Fig.~6), outcome reward,
policy reward. The ratings by work-up class (the classes of Fig.~2b) go to ED Fig.~5.
\item 20--30 admissions solved by neurologists through the same interface: each model read against
the neurologist of the case (same diagnosis, the neurologist's tests ordered), the neurologists' own
share of required tests, and tests per case. The neurologists' share of required tests says whether
the policies ask for more than neurologists do; tests per case says whether the trained model orders
about as many tests as they do.
\end{itemize}

\structure
\begin{enumerate}
\item Design of the two studies (100 words).
\item Ratings against the scores, and of each agent (150).
\item Physicians against the models (100).
\end{enumerate}

\artefacts
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fig.~4} (below, revised 18 Nov after the second critical review, awaiting approval): a,
agreement of each score with the ratings; b, the blind rating of each agent; c, the three models
against neurologists on the same admissions. The study design is in the legend and Methods.
\textbf{Supp.\ Table~9}: agreement; \textbf{Supp.\ Table~12}: the numbers of c; \textbf{Supp.\
Table~14}: the ratings of b and of ED Fig.~5 (ratings by work-up class).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{R6. The gain comes from the reward, not from the prompt, imitation or a panel of agents}\label{sec:cost}

\role{Claim C5. Answers ``is it more than a good prompt?'' and ``would a multi-agent system do the
same without training?'' on the same base model, and gives the compute each option needs.}

\content
\begin{itemize}
\item The same untrained Qwen3.5-9B used in three other ways. Rules in the prompt: the prompt states
in words what the policy score checks (the tests a diagnosis needs before it is given, no test
without a reason, the order, when to stop), with nothing specific to a case or condition. Supervised
fine-tuning on reference episodes of the training cases. A multi-agent panel in the style of MAI-DxO:
five roles (hypothesis, test chooser, challenger, stewardship, checklist) discuss each step, then one
typed action goes to the same tools.
\item Larger untrained models, alone, with the rules in the prompt and as a panel: what scale buys,
and a check that the panel works when the model is strong, so the 9B panel is not a weak opponent.
\item Measures that are not the policy score: correct diagnoses, required tests ordered, ordered tests
the policy asks for. Tokens per case as the compute. The panel's work-ups are also rated blind
(Fig.~4b).
\end{itemize}

\structure
\begin{enumerate}
\item The same model without reinforcement learning (170 words).
\item Larger models and compute (130).
\end{enumerate}

\artefacts
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fig.~6} (below, redrawn 18 Nov after the second critical review, awaiting approval): a,
Qwen3.5-9B used in five ways; b, larger models. \emph{Why:} the earlier figure compared the trained
model with untrained ones on the policy score it was trained on; this one asks where the gain comes
from, on measures every agent can be judged by. \textbf{Supp.\ Table~4} (numbers of the figure);
\textbf{Supp.\ Table~10} (tokens, wall time, GPU memory); \textbf{Supp.\ Table~7} (models).
\end{itemize}
